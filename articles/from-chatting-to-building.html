From chatting to building

A workshop asking for AI ideas only lands with the people already building. Everyone else needs something different.

Rahul Sharma 20 October 2026 2 min read

When a company decides to “experiment with AI”, someone books a workshop, there are sticky notes, and someone asks, “So, what could we do with AI?” Then everyone stares at the wall.

The trouble is that the room holds four different audiences. The Wall wants AI nowhere near their job, and isn’t a fringe: 29% of workers admit to undermining their company’s AI strategy1 (though some of them are quietly using it at home2). The Skeptic tried it once, got a mediocre answer and decided it was hype. The Chatter uses it daily, but beside the real work. The Builder has started changing how the work gets done.

A workshop asking for ideas only lands with Builders. A hack day does a bit better, but mostly turns Skeptics into Chatters and stops there. That’s where most people already are: Section found 73.5% of US knowledge workers use AI often, but only for one-off tasks. Just 5.5% have built it into how they work.3

Last year I built a scoring app for the junior cricket team I coach. I’m not a developer and it has no back end, but [what it does / how long it took / one thing that surprised you]. It taught me more about these tools than any course would have.

For the Wall and the Skeptic, a personal project like that is the way in. You can stuff it up without it affecting anyone. You care about the result, so you keep going until it works. And shipping something real turns “this is scary” into “oh, I can use this”.

For the Chatter, it’s the workflow question. Don’t ask, “Where could we use AI?” Ask, “Walk me through how you actually do this.” Having built something, they look at the client report or the spreadsheet nobody likes maintaining differently, and start noticing the copying and pasting, the repeated decisions, the expertise stuck in one person’s head. That’s the step from chatting to building.

Structure makes it stick: a set-up session, weekly check-ins, and a show-and-tell in week four. Nobody wants to turn up with nothing, and a Skeptic watching a colleague demo something real is more persuasive than any vendor.

At that point you’re not experimenting with AI. You’re experimenting with how the business works, which is where BCG finds the real value.4

So next time someone books the workshop, skip the sticky notes. Ask everyone to bring something they built at the weekend, even if it’s only to prove it doesn’t work.
