Everyone has the tools. Nothing has changed.

The gap isn’t access, and it isn’t training. It’s that nobody’s actual work has changed shape.

Rahul Sharma 29 September 2026 4 min read

Your firm has the tools. There is a licence attached to every account, somebody has expensed a pro subscription, and a partner has almost certainly forwarded an article about agents in the last fortnight.

So here is the question worth sitting with: name one thing about how your team works that is different from a year ago. Not one thing someone tried. One thing that changed and stayed changed.

For most firms the honest answer is nothing, and that is not a failure of enthusiasm. IBM’s 2026 CEO Study found 86% of employees have AI access, and only about a quarter use it regularly.1 The tools arrived. The work did not move.

86% have access. A quarter use it. Nobody planned for that gap.

Access was never the constraint

The assumption underneath most AI rollouts is that the hard part is getting the tool in front of people. Buy the licences, run a session, appoint some champions, and adoption follows.

It doesn’t, because the distance between having a capable tool and having somewhere to put it is much larger than it looks. MIT’s work on the divide between firms that get value from generative AI and the majority that don’t lands in roughly the same place.2 The separation is not the technology. Everyone has the same models.

What separates them is whether anyone’s work actually changed shape. Not sped up at the margins — changed. A step that used to take a person a morning now takes twenty minutes, and the morning went somewhere else.

What “using AI” usually means

Ask a team how they use AI and you get a fairly consistent list. Better-worded emails. First drafts of documents that arguably did not need writing. Summaries of meetings nobody was going to read. Someone in the team is genuinely brilliant with it and does something clever on their own time.

None of that is nothing. All of it is invisible at the level of the business. It is personal productivity, distributed unevenly and never written down, which means it leaves when the person does.

The test I use is blunt: if every one of those uses stopped tomorrow, would a client notice? Would a deadline move? If not, you have adoption in the survey sense and nothing in the operating sense.

Why more training doesn’t fix it

The standard response to a flat adoption number is more education. A lunch-and-learn, a prompt library on the intranet, a longer list of use cases.

The problem is that knowledge with nowhere to go decays in about a week. People come out of a good session genuinely motivated, return to a full inbox, and the new thing loses to the urgent thing every single time. A month later they can tell you what a prompt is and point at nothing they built.

What people need is not more understanding of AI in general. It is to finish one thing with it, once, on work that is actually theirs.

The unit of change is one person, one real task

The thing that reliably works is unglamorous. Take one person. Take one task they do most weeks, that they own, and that quietly annoys them. Work on that task, with the tool, in short sessions spread over weeks rather than one long workshop — because the learning happens in the gaps, when they hit something that doesn’t work and have to get round it.

Then make them show it to somebody. The showing matters more than it should. A demo to colleagues does something a certificate never will: it makes the change real, it makes it repeatable, and it recruits the next three people without you having to sell anything.

Do that four or five times in a firm and you have something you did not have before. Not a strategy — evidence. Evidence is what tells you whether the bigger investment is worth making, and in what order.

The part nobody wants to say out loud

There is a harder version of this problem sitting underneath the productivity one, and it comes up in almost every conversation I have with partners at professional services firms.

Juniors used to learn the work by doing the grunt work. Reformatting the file, chasing the missing document, drafting the thing that gets marked up in red. That grunt work is precisely what the tools are now good at, which means the cheapest thing to automate is also the apprenticeship.

I don’t think the answer is to protect the grunt work on purpose. But I do think a firm that automates it without deciding how its people will now develop has quietly traded a five-year problem for a one-year saving. That is a leadership decision, not an IT one, and it is worth making deliberately rather than by accident.

Which is the real argument here. The question is not whether your firm is using AI. Almost certainly it is, a bit, in ways nobody has written down. The question is whether anything about how the work gets done is different, and whether you chose the difference.
