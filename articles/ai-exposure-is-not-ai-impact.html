AI exposure isn’t the same thing as AI impact

Exposure is a poor proxy for extinction. Technology changes the composition of a job before it eliminates it.

Rahul Sharma 29 September 2026 2 min read

Much of the AI jobs debate asks which roles are most “exposed”. I’m sceptical the term tells us much. Yale’s Budget Lab found no discernible US labour market disruption in the 33 months after ChatGPT, and that similarly exposed jobs adopted AI at very different rates.1

Today’s models are over-specced for most work, yet economy-wide productivity gains haven’t arrived. A study of 25,000 Danish workers found chatbots saved them about 3% of their time, with no effect on earnings or hours.2

The model isn’t the bottleneck. The organisation is.

McKinsey found that redesigning workflows mattered most for getting a return from AI, yet only 21% of companies had done any.3

Caught in the middle are senior ICs and managers, guarding quality as review volumes rise while learning the tools and rethinking workflows on top of the day job. AI makes producing work cheaper and checking it more expensive. Stanford and BetterUp found each piece of AI “workslop” takes nearly two hours to fix;4 77% of AI users say it has added to their workload.5 And that’s before we get to burnout.

In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton said we should stop training radiologists. A decade on, demand for them is growing.6 After the spreadsheet arrived, the US lost 400,000 bookkeeping clerks over 35 years and gained 600,000 accountants.7

AI will eliminate some roles. But exposure is a poor proxy for extinction: technology changes the composition of a job before it eliminates it. The better question is: “What happens to the work when the cost of producing the first draft approaches zero?”

That’s where the real disruption starts.
