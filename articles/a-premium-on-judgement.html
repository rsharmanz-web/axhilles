A premium on judgement rather than time

The real question isn’t how to reprice the work. It’s what to sell once the work gets cheap.

Rahul Sharma 13 October 2026 2 min read

Professional services have long priced on a simple model: a person spends time, and you charge for the time. Not because clients value time, but because it’s easy to measure.

AI is shrinking the time, and clients have noticed. KPMG pressed its own auditor to pass on its AI savings and won a 14% fee cut.1

The standard answer is outcome pricing: stop selling hours and charge for results. It has been the future of professional services for about twenty years. In practice, outcomes take years to show up, depend as much on the client as the adviser, and rarely have a single cause. McKinsey says a quarter of its fees are now tied to outcomes,2 but few firms have McKinsey’s appetite for risk. In law, 90% of the money still flows through hourly billing, and rates rose 7.4% last year.3

So the real question isn’t how to reprice the work. It’s what to sell once the work gets cheap.

The capacity AI frees up can go into things that were never economically viable. A contract review a mid-sized business could never justify. Compliance monitoring that runs all year rather than once. A strategy engagement that tests ten options rather than defending one. A&O Shearman now licenses its contract-review tool to clients as software, a law firm selling a product rather than its people’s time.4

What stays scarce is judgement: knowing which of the ten options to back, and when the draft is wrong. The challenge is building offers around that judgement inside firms whose pyramids, utilisation targets and partner pay were all designed to sell hours.

Telcos didn’t survive the end of long-distance charges by finding a cleverer way to price a minute. They started selling something else.
