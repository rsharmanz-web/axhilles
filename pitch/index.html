Chapter 01 — Context

Context

Take a dip

When Achilles was born, his mother Thetis did something every parent's probably wished they could do at some point. She took him down to the River Styx and dipped him in it, and wherever that water touched, nothing could hurt him. Swords just bounced off. Arrows too.

There was exactly one place the water never reached — the heel she was holding him by. That spot stayed exactly as vulnerable as anyone else's.

Superhuman

With the story of Achilles, people usually default to the idea that “Achilles was strong, but he had a flaw.” For years he walked through the Trojan War completely untouchable — not lucky, not careful, just structurally impossible to hurt, almost everywhere.

He wasn't superhuman despite the heel. He was superhuman and he had exactly one spot that worked like everyone else's whole body — both true, at the same time.

Based on what I've experienced over the past couple of years applying AI in anger at Xero to transform a team and a product experience, it feels very much like that — on one hand we've got a new way of doing things that feels superhuman, but at the same time, it becomes a flaw that you're constantly trying to address.

The question isn't whether it's powerful. It obviously is. The question is whether you know where your heel is before you find out the hard way.

Xero — up in the air

I'll start with Xero, seeing I've lived and breathed it for the past five years.

Twenty years ago, accounting software lived on one desktop, in the back office. One licence, one machine, one person who could open the file.

Xero didn't just move that software onto the internet — it used the cloud to move the numbers into a virtual room where accountants, business owners and the financial system (banks and tax agencies) could all stand in together, live, on the same day.

With that, the job on the other side of the software evolved. It didn't shrink. It moved — less time keying things in, more time telling you what the numbers actually mean for you.

Design — in the air

Design went through almost the same thing, a bit later.

A design file used to live on one person's laptop. Feedback meant an email with a PDF attached, and everyone hoping they were looking at the same version.

Figma put everyone's cursor on the same canvas, in the browser, at once. And the nice surprise wasn't that the software got cleverer — it's that reviewing the work stopped being an awkward scheduled meeting and became something continuous, something the client could watch happen, live.

Neither of those is really a story about better software. They're both about who's in the room, and when — and every time that changes, the job on the other side of it changes shape too.

The shift

We're right at the start of that same kind of shift with AI. It's just faster, and touching more of the work at once than either of those did.

Economists have a name for tools that do this to an entire economy, not just one industry — a “general purpose technology.” (Side bar: that's not what GPT in ChatGPT stands for; it's Generative Pre-trained Transformer.) It's a list that includes breakthroughs like electricity, the internet and mobile — things that don't just get a little better at one job, they end up changing the shape of most jobs around them.

OpenAI's researchers estimate that somewhere around 46% of jobs could have at least half their tasks meaningfully touched by this generation of AI, once the tools built around it catch up to what the models can already do.

And like these shifts we're navigating, the impact isn't universal — with outsized impact in some areas and skills over others.

Back to the heel: the strength

Which brings us back to Achilles — the strong part first.

In 2023, Harvard Business School and Boston Consulting Group basically ran an experiment to find out how strong this actually gets, and where it stops. 758 consultants, real client-style work, some using AI, some not.

On the work AI was genuinely good at, the difference was enormous. 12.2% more tasks finished. A quarter faster. Quality rated 40% higher by the people judging it.

But hand the same tool a task just slightly outside what it's actually good at, and the story flips.

On those tasks, the people using AI did about 19 percentage points worse than the people who didn't touch it at all. Not because the tool loudly failed and everyone noticed. Because people quietly trusted it exactly where they shouldn't have, and switched their own judgement off.

In the industry, researchers are calling this the “jagged frontier.” It doesn't run along job titles, or even whole jobs.

It zig-zags right through the middle of the work itself, and you can't see it from the outside — you only find it by testing where it actually sits on your work. Not the average business's work. Yours.

Part 2: the choice

Which brings me to the second story, because as well as the contrast between being superhuman and being flawed, the story of Achilles is also about a choice he needs to make.

The Greeks were big on fate, and for Achilles, his mother tells him there are two on the table. Stay home, live a long, comfortable, thoroughly unremarkable life, and be forgotten. Or go to Troy, fight, and die young — but be sung about for the rest of time.

He picks Troy. Short and remarkable, over long and safe.

The catch

Here's the catch, though, and it's the bit I actually want to leave you with.

Achilles' choice only works as a choice because staying home was genuinely, actually safe. Nobody was coming for him in his own house.

But if I'm honest, that's the challenge facing business owners today. “Staying home” — not touching any of this, waiting to see how it shakes out — isn't actually the safe option anymore, because the market moves whether you engage with it or not. Your competitors reaching for the same tools makes standing still quietly risky too, even though it doesn't feel like a decision at all.

So you don't get Achilles' deal.

There's no long, comfortable, forgettable version sitting there waiting for you if you'd just rather not. The only real choice left is how you engage.

Dual track

In some ways that makes the choice a little easier.

The tools underneath all this are moving fast enough that a two- or three-year “AI strategy” is really just a nice story you're telling yourself anyway. So instead of one big bet, run two streams in parallel.

Track one: what can you actually put in people's hands next month — on real client work, not a pilot tucked away where nobody's watching?

Track two: what do you need built — capability and process-wise — by roughly twelve months out, about as far as anyone can honestly plan before the technology evolves.

That's the choice most service businesses are already making, whether they've noticed it as a choice or not.

How work is changing

01 From hours to outcomes Clients increasingly question time-based billing when AI compresses how long a task actually takes.
02 From generalist output to judgement The parts of the job hardest to automate — judgement, taste, client relationships — become the differentiators.
03 From tools bought to capability built Off-the-shelf AI tools without process and training around them rarely move the needle. The businesses that pull ahead treat this as an operating model change, not a software purchase.

Chapter 02 — Maximising AI Internally

Maximising AI Internally

Draft — working notes, refine before use

When we go into a business — an accounting practice, a law firm, a design studio, another consultancy — the goal isn't to hand over a tool. It's to get people from not knowing what these tools do to genuinely able to experiment with them, then build the connective tissue that lets that experimentation compound instead of staying scattered.

01

Fundamentals

Before anyone touches a live workflow, we get people to a working baseline — what these tools are actually good at, what they're not, and enough hands-on practice on a low-stakes side project that the first time it matters, they're not starting from zero.

02

Infrastructure

These tools rarely fail because the model isn't good enough — they fail because the context they need is scattered across email, chat and file storage. We help set up that connective layer so the tools have something to work with.

03

Getting people started

We don't roll AI out firm-wide on day one. We pick one team, get it genuinely working there — structured, week by week — then expand ring by ring: the next team, then the next, then the wider business.

Chapter 03 — AI Change Management Strategy (working title)

AI Change Management Strategy

Draft — working notes, refine before use

This is the consulting engagement itself: assessing where a business actually stands, then designing — with the business, not for it — the roadmap that gets it from there to where it wants to be.

01

Assess

We start with an honest baseline: what tools are already in use, what they're being used for, what's actually working, what's just noise — before we talk about anything new.

02

Design

From there, we work backwards from the customer's experience across the whole engagement lifecycle — how work is won, delivered and closed — to find where AI can meaningfully change the shape of the work.

03

Mobilise

Every experiment gets a way to check whether it actually worked, and a way to feed what's learned back into the next one — so the business ends up with a system that compounds.

Chapter 04 — Build

Build

Draft — working notes, refine before use

Some of what comes out of this work is a repeatable product, not just a one-off project — and figuring out which ones are worth building out further is its own discipline.

01

Prototype

We build small and fast — usually inside a single team, solving a real problem they already have — before deciding whether something is worth investing in further.

02

Productise

The tell is usually simple: a tool built to solve one narrow, internal problem turns out to be valuable enough on its own that people want it as a standalone product.

03

Scale & sell

Not everything is worth scaling. Some tools quietly get absorbed as the big AI platforms add native skills — so part of the discipline is being honest about where the durable advantage is judgement and domain expertise.
