## chapter 01

Context

AI isn't a future consideration for service businesses — it's already reshaping how work gets scoped, delivered and priced. The businesses that treat it as a side project will spend the next few years catching up to the ones that treat it as core infrastructure.

How work is changing

  • From hours to outcomes. Clients increasingly question time-based billing when AI compresses how long a task actually takes.
  • From generalist output to judgement. The parts of the job hardest to automate — judgement, taste, client relationships — become the differentiators.
  • From tools bought to capability built. Off-the-shelf AI tools without process and training around them rarely move the needle. The businesses that pull ahead treat this as an operating model change, not a software purchase.

Our point of view

We've spent our careers running AI and technology transformation programmes inside large enterprises across NZ, Australia, the UK and US — seeing first-hand which pilots scaled, which quietly died, and why.

The pattern we kept seeing: the businesses that got real value weren't the ones that moved fastest — they were the ones that built fluency (understanding, control and confidence) into how their people worked, rather than bolting tools on top of old processes.

That's the gap we now close for small and medium service businesses: the enterprise-grade thinking, without the enterprise price tag or timelines.

Where this comes from

Before Axhilles, one of the last things I worked on was how Xero should commercialise AI — a question a much larger team of consultants was already modelling in parallel with us. We ran it with two people instead of five, because we kept the question simple and kept coming back to it: forget the model for a second — what does the customer actually see, and where does this show up in their day-to-day work?

Starting from the customer's experience and working backwards, rather than starting from the technology, is still the first thing we do on every engagement.

## chapter 02

Maximising AI Internally

[ DRAFT — working notes, refine before use ]
When we go into a business — an accounting practice, a law firm, a design studio, another consultancy — the goal isn't to hand over a tool. It's to get people from not knowing what these tools do to genuinely able to experiment with them, then build the connective tissue that lets that experimentation compound instead of staying scattered.

01

Fundamentals

Before anyone touches a live workflow, we get people to a working baseline — what these tools are actually good at, what they're not, and enough hands-on practice on a low-stakes side project (not a live client file) that the first time it matters, they're not starting from zero.

02

Infrastructure

These tools rarely fail because the model isn't good enough — they fail because the context they need is scattered across email, chat and file storage with no consistent, permissioned way to pull it together. We help set up that connective layer so the tools have something to work with.

03

Getting people started

We don't roll AI out firm-wide on day one. We pick one team, get it genuinely working there — structured, week by week, with a visible marker of progress — then expand ring by ring: the next team, then the next, then the wider business.

## chapter 03

AI Change Management Strategy (working title)

[ DRAFT — working notes, refine before use ]
This is the consulting engagement itself: assessing where a business actually stands, then designing — with the business, not for it — the roadmap that gets it from there to where it wants to be.

01

Assess

We start with an honest baseline: what tools are already in use, what they're being used for, what's actually working, what's just noise — before we talk about anything new.

02

Design

From there, we work backwards from the customer's experience across the whole engagement lifecycle — how work is won, delivered and closed — to find where AI can meaningfully change the shape of the work, not just speed up a step nobody cared about.

03

Mobilise

Every experiment gets a way to check whether it actually worked, and a way to feed what's learned back into the next one — so the business ends up with a system that compounds, not a folder of one-off projects nobody can integrate.

## chapter 04

Build

[ DRAFT — working notes, refine before use ]
Some of what comes out of this work is a repeatable product, not just a one-off project — and figuring out which ones are worth building out further is its own discipline.

01

Prototype

We build small and fast — usually inside a single team, solving a real problem they already have — before deciding whether something is worth investing in further.

02

Productise

The tell is usually simple: a tool built to solve one narrow, internal problem turns out to be valuable enough on its own that people want it as a standalone product. Recognising that moment, and knowing what it takes to turn a one-off build into something repeatable, is where a lot of the value sits.

03

Scale & sell

Not everything is worth scaling. Some tools quietly get absorbed as the big AI platforms add native skills — so part of the discipline is being honest about where the durable advantage is judgement and domain expertise, not the tool itself.
