# 2. Contact

The cold call, word for word.

**The only goal of the call is a booked Discovery.** Not a sale, not a pitch, not a "let me send you
some information". Thirty minutes in the calendar, confirmed before you hang up.

---

## Before the block

Five minutes of setup protects ninety minutes of calling.

- Call list printed or on screen, 30 rows, one vertical, mobiles found, triggers written in
- Calendly open with three specific slots picked out for the next four working days
- Notes doc open with the tracker columns, so logging takes seconds
- Stand up, phone in hand. It changes your voice more than you would expect
- Recording on and announced. Review three calls from every block

---

## The call structure

Six beats, about four minutes when it goes well.

```
1. Permission open        20 sec   Earn the right to keep talking
2. Reason for the call    30 sec   Their trigger + our credibility + a pain hypothesis
3. One diagnostic         60 sec   A real question. Then shut up
4. Light qualification    60 sec   Task, scope, who decides
5. The ask                30 sec   Discovery, framed as low risk
6. Lock it in             30 sec   Specific time, invite sent while you are still on the phone
```

### 1. Permission open

Do not disguise the call. Naming it earns more goodwill than any warm-up.

> "Sarah, it's Rahul Sharma from Axhilles, here in Auckland. I'll be upfront — this is a cold call.
> Can I have thirty seconds to tell you why I picked up the phone, and then you can tell me to go
> away?"

Almost nobody says no to thirty seconds. Then honour it: thirty seconds means thirty seconds.

**Variants**

- *Referred:* "James at Baker Tilly suggested I call you — have you got two minutes?"
- *Pre-touched:* "I sent you a short email yesterday about the automation role you're advertising.
  Probably buried — can I give you the thirty-second version?"
- *Voicemail returned:* "Thanks for calling back. Let me be quick about why I rang."

### 2. Reason for the call

Three ingredients: **their trigger**, **why us**, **the pain hypothesis**. Their trigger comes first
because it is the only part that is about them.

> "I saw you're hiring an automation lead. I ran the AI design research team at Xero — we took AI
> features from prototype to global release — and I now work with accounting practices on exactly
> that problem. The pattern I keep finding is that everyone has the tools, a couple of people use
> them brilliantly, and nothing changes at the level of the actual work. I wanted to find out
> whether that's what it looks like inside your firm."

Why this works: specific to them, credentialed without a CV recital, and the hypothesis is
falsifiable — which makes it a conversation instead of a claim.

### 3. One diagnostic question

Ask one. Then stop talking, even through the silence.

- "How are your people actually using AI today — is it a few individuals doing their own thing, or is
  there a plan behind it?"
- "If you had to name the one process in the firm that eats the most hours for the least value, what
  comes to mind?"
- "When someone in the team asks 'can we use AI for this?' — who answers?"

The second question is the workhorse. It surfaces a named workflow, which is what both Baseline and
Alpha need, and it makes them the expert in the conversation.

### 4. Light qualification

Three facts, conversationally, not as a form. These are the same facts Chax collects from web
visitors, so the data stays consistent wherever the lead came from.

| Fact | How to ask |
|---|---|
| **The task** | "Walk me through what that looks like now — who touches it and in what order?" |
| **The size of it** | "Roughly how many hours a week does that swallow across the team?" |
| **Scope and authority** | "Is that just your team, or the whole firm? And if you decided to try something, is that your call or does it go to the partners?" |

Do not qualify further on the phone. Discovery is where depth happens; over-qualifying on a cold
call is how you talk yourself out of the meeting.

### 5. The ask

Make it small, specific and explicitly not a pitch.

> "Here's what I'd suggest. I do a thirty-minute Discovery call — no deck, no pitch. We take that one
> process, and I'll tell you straight whether AI is actually the answer, or whether it's a process
> problem wearing an AI costume. Worst case you get a free opinion from someone who's built this
> stuff. Would Thursday at 10, or Friday at 2 work better?"

Two named slots, not "when suits?" — an open question makes them do work, and they will defer.

### 6. Lock it in

- Confirm the email address out loud, spelled back
- Send the Calendly confirmation or calendar invite while still on the call
- "You'll see it land now — can you confirm you've got it?"
- Set the expectation: "I'll send one short thing to read beforehand, and I'll ask you two questions
  in the email. Two lines back is plenty."
- Log the outcome before you dial the next number

## Segment talk tracks

Same structure, different pain hypothesis. Swap the middle of beat 2.

**Accounting / audit practice**

> "Every practice I talk to is carrying the same three things: compliance work that has to happen but
> doesn't pay well, capacity that runs out before the work does, and juniors who used to learn by
> doing the grunt work that AI is now good at. The third one is the interesting one — it changes how
> your people develop, not just how fast the file closes."

**Law firm**

> "The firms I speak to are all working out where AI touches client work and where it absolutely
> cannot. Usually the honest answer is that the win isn't in the advice at all, it's in everything
> around it — intake, precedents, file notes, the first draft nobody wants to write."

**Insurance broking**

> "Broking runs on renewals, submissions and claims admin, and most of it is moving information
> between formats. That's the least glamorous and highest-value place to start."

**Agency / consultancy**

> "You're selling thinking by the hour while your clients are asking why AI hasn't made you cheaper.
> The firms getting ahead of that are changing what they sell, not just how they produce it."

**Baker Tilly or other partner** — different ask entirely: an introduction, not a sale.

> "I'm not calling to sell you anything. I work with your kind of client on AI — the practical end,
> not strategy decks — and I'd like fifteen minutes to show you what it looks like, so you've got
> something concrete to offer when a client asks you about AI. If it's useful you can point one
> client at it and watch. If it isn't, you've lost fifteen minutes."

## Objection handling

Pattern for all of them: **acknowledge, reframe with something specific, ask a question.** Never
argue. Every response ends with a question, so the ball stays in play.

**"We're already using AI / everyone's got ChatGPT."**
> "Most firms are, and that's exactly the pattern — 86% of employees have access, about a quarter use
> it regularly. Access isn't the hard part. Can I ask: is anyone's actual work different this month
> than it was six months ago, or is it mostly better emails?"

**"I don't have time."**
> "Completely fair, and I'll be quick. Should I stop, or is it worth thirty more seconds?"
> — If they say stop: "Understood. When's genuinely better, three weeks?" Book the *call about the
> call* and honour it exactly.

**"Just send me some information."**
> "Happy to, and I will. But most of what I'd send is generic, and you'd be reading about other
> people's problems. Give me one process that's annoying you and I'll send you something about that
> instead — what comes to mind?"
> — Send it regardless, and put them on the [nurture cadence](4-follow-up.md). "Send me information"
> means "not now", not "no".

**"No budget."**
> "That's usually the right answer for AI projects, because most of them are priced like software
> builds. The way in is $750 a head for a month — one person, one real thing built. It's a
> professional development line, not a capex conversation. Is the constraint money, or is it that
> nobody's convinced there's a return yet?"

**"Our software vendor is adding AI features."**
> "They are, and you should use them. I spent years building exactly those features at Xero, and
> here's the thing from the inside: the vendor optimises the steps inside their product. The waste in
> your firm is in the gaps between products, and no vendor is going there. Which of your handoffs
> between systems hurts most?"

**"Client confidentiality / data security."**
> "Correct instinct, and it's the first thing we set up, not an afterthought. Participants use their
> own approved tooling, and the intake form specifically asks what can't go near a public model.
> Who owns that call at your firm — is it you, or is there an IT or risk partner I should include?"
> — This objection is often a buying signal in disguise: they have thought about it.

**"Our people won't use it."**
> "Usually true, and it's why training-only approaches fail. The programme is built the other way
> round: each person brings a live piece of their own work, and by week four they present what they
> built. Nobody's learning in the abstract. Who on your team would actually be keen?"

**"How is this different from a course?"**
> "A course gives you knowledge. This gives you a thing that exists — thirty days, a two-hour setup,
> weekly one-to-ones on your real work, and a showcase where you demo what you built. If nothing got
> built, it didn't work."

**"We tried something like this and it flopped."**
> "Tell me what happened — genuinely, that's more useful to me than my own pitch." Then listen. This
> is the best qualifying conversation available; a burnt buyer who explains why is closer to buying
> than a polite stranger.

**"Call me after year end / busy season."**
> "Makes sense. I'll call you the week of the 14th. Quick one before I go — when it quiets down, what
> is the thing you'd want fixed before the next one?" Lock the date in the tracker and name it on the
> callback: "You told me to ring back this week."

## Voicemail

Two voicemails per sequence, maximum. Twenty seconds each, and never repeat the same message.

**First**

> "Sarah, Rahul Sharma, Axhilles, 021 XXX XXXX. I'm calling about the automation role you're
> advertising — I ran AI design research at Xero and I work with accounting practices on that exact
> problem. Nothing to sell on this call, just a question. 021 XXX XXXX. Thanks."

**Second, a week later, new information**

> "Sarah, Rahul again from Axhilles. I won't keep chasing. I sent you one thing — a four-week
> programme, $750 a head, where people build something real instead of reading about AI. It's in your
> inbox under 'Building a habit'. If it's not for you, ignore both and no hard feelings. 021 XXX XXXX."

Number twice, at the start and the end. Slow down for it.

## Gatekeepers

The receptionist at a 40-person practice knows everything. Treat them as a source, not an obstacle.

> "Morning, I'm hoping you can help me. I'm trying to work out who at the firm would own a question
> about how the team uses AI in their day-to-day work — is that Sarah, or someone else?"

- Be straight if asked whether it is sales: "It is, yes — I'm hoping for two minutes with whoever owns
  that. Is that a lost cause?" Honesty gets transferred more often than a script does.
- Ask for the best time rather than a transfer: "When's she usually at her desk?" Then call then.
- Get their name and use it next time. Three calls in, they are helping you on purpose.

---

## After every call

Log it immediately, in the row you were already looking at.

- Outcome code: `connected` / `voicemail` / `gatekeeper` / `no-answer` / `wrong-person` / `not-now` /
  `booked` / `disqualified`
- The exact words they used about their pain — verbatim, because you will reuse them in the follow-up
- Score the lead ([`3-rank.md`](3-rank.md))
- Set the next action and its date. **No lead leaves a call block without a next action.**

---

## Improving the script

The script is a hypothesis. Run it like one.

- **Track objections by tally.** The most frequent objection each month is the thing to rewrite next.
- **Change one thing at a time.** One opener across 30 dials, a different one across the next 30.
  Never two variables in one block.
- **Listen to three recordings a week**, for one thing only: where did they go quiet, and where did
  you talk too long.
- **Retire lines that only work for you.** If a line needs your exact delivery to land, it will not
  survive being handed to anyone else later.
